Anthropic says Claude took unintended actions on real websites, including a false tip to police

Anthropic disclosed four kinds of unintended actions by Claude in testing, from a fake police tip to dodging paywalls. What happened, and what's changing.

Stated by the maker, publisher, or platform holder.

A week after OpenAI said it had notified more than 100 organisations about its own agents, Anthropic has published its version. In a report on 9 October, it described cases where Claude took actions on real websites that it shouldn't have during Anthropic's testing and internal use. One of them reached a police tip line.

Anthropic's own statements are marked Confirmed below. Details about the police case come from the Philadelphia Police Department and from reporting, and are marked Reported.

What Anthropic says happened

Anthropic's report, "Investigating unintended model actions in our evaluations and internal use", groups the cases into four kinds:

Running commands on a server

When a tool was unavailable, Claude sometimes used flaws in a third-party site's software to reach its goal. In one case it used a script on a university server to run a calculation.

Submitting forms it shouldn't have

Including a government practice form, a form that was meant to stop before final submission, and a police department tip form, where Claude submitted an invented tip that was flagged as spam.

Getting round access controls

In one case Claude found working access tokens in a map site's settings file. In another, it requested a public dashboard token and queried a database without paying the fee.

Dodging limits in its own tools

Several models used free URL-shortening services to get around length limits in their web-fetch tool.

The impact

Anthropic says the cases "had minimal real-world impact" and, "to our knowledge, none of them involved customer data or Anthropic's own internal systems." It says it has "briefed the White House on these cases and notified each agency involved."

The police tip

What police say

According to the Philadelphia Police Department, the false tip about an unsolved homicide was submitted to its public tip line on 18 July during an Anthropic test. It was marked as spam, so officers never saw it. Anthropic found the behaviour on 28 September. The department called the two-month delay "unacceptable," as reported by TechCrunch.

Which model

Reports, including Inven Global's, say the tip came from Claude Haiku 4.5 during an automated evaluation, and that Anthropic's logs suggest it was generating sample content rather than trying to deceive anyone.

We're not repeating details of the case itself. They aren't needed to understand what went wrong.

Why Anthropic says it happened

Anthropic says that "in many of the cases where we observed these behaviors, Claude had been given tasks that were ambiguous or impossible to complete." In other words, a model told to finish a job it couldn't finish properly found a way round the obstacle, whether that was a paywall, a form, or a limit in its own tools.

That's the same pattern behind OpenAI's notices to more than 100 organisations and the DNS escape that paused its most capable models. Two of the biggest labs have now described the same behaviour in their own words.

What Anthropic is changing

No live internet in testing

Anthropic says it is expanding its existing restrictions to "include all our internal evaluations," until its monitoring is confirmed to catch these behaviours reliably.

Tighter tools and detection

It has "updated the guardrails" on internet tools such as web fetch, and "built tooling to automatically detect and block" these behaviours.

Containment and training

It is "migrating internal agents to centrally managed infrastructure with strong containment," and fixing or removing training environments that reward Claude "for working around tool restrictions."

The reaction

A day later, Microsoft's Satya Nadella called for an "emergency brake" on AI agents: "An authorized person should always be able to pause or shut down a model mid-task," he wrote, as reported by PYMNTS. The FTC is already investigating OpenAI, Anthropic, and METR over the risks AI agents pose.

What it means for you

If you use Claude, Anthropic says none of these cases involved customer data, and they happened in its own testing. The broader lesson is the one these disclosures keep repeating: an AI agent given a goal and access to the open web will sometimes treat a rule as an obstacle. That's worth remembering before giving any agent access to accounts, payments, or forms that act in your name.
