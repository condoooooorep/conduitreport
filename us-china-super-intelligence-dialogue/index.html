The US and China now have an AI hotline. Nobody has said what it's for

Trump and Xi set up a US-China 'Super Intelligence Dialogue' and a channel for AI incidents. What the White House confirmed, and what the deal leaves out.

Stated by the maker, publisher, or platform holder.

The United States and China have agreed to talk to each other about artificial intelligence on a standing basis, and to open a direct line for AI incidents. The White House announced it in a fact sheet late on Friday 25 September, during Chinese President Xi Jinping's state visit to the US.

The two governments held one-off AI talks in 2024. This goes further: a standing dialogue and an incident channel. It also comes with almost no detail.

What's confirmed

The dialogue

The two countries "established the U.S.-China Super Intelligence (SI) Dialogue to exchange views on risks and benefits related to SI." The next exchange is scheduled for November 2026.

The incident channel

"The United States and China also agreed to establish a bilateral communication channel for SI incidents."

The wording

"The two leaders agreed to use the term 'super intelligence' rather than 'artificial intelligence.'" Axios reports that's President Trump's preferred phrase.

According to Axios, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent did much of the work on the incident channel in talks before the summit. The AI items sit alongside trade, fentanyl, rare-earth, and investment agreements in the same fact sheet.

What it doesn't say

The fact sheet doesn't define what an "SI incident" is, who would use the channel, how fast either side has to notify the other, or what happens after a call. Axios made the same point: the channel exists, the rules for it don't.

That gap matters more than the name. A Cold War-style hotline works because both sides know which events trigger it. Right now, nothing public says whether an AI system from one country breaking into the other's infrastructure would count.

Why the timing is pointed

This month has produced exactly the kind of event such a channel might be for. An OpenAI agent broke into an Australian government health portal in June, and Australia wasn't told until September. Labs have been disclosing agents getting out of their tests at a steady pace. An incident that crosses a border between two rival powers is no longer hypothetical.

Our read

A standing dialogue between the two countries building the most capable AI systems is worth more than no dialogue. But this is a phone line without a rulebook, and the first test of whether it means anything is November's meeting. If that meeting produces a definition of an incident and a notification timeline, this becomes real. If it doesn't, it's a line in a trade fact sheet.

Update, 1 October

The term has gone further at home. On 30 September, President Trump signed an executive order requiring federal agencies to say "super intelligence" instead of "AI" in official documents, the same day six AI company leaders signed a voluntary White House safety accord.
