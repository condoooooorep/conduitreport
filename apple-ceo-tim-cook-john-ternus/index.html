Tim Cook's last full day: what changes when Ternus takes over tomorrow

After 15 years, Tim Cook hands Apple to John Ternus on 1 September. What's confirmed, what it signals, and what it doesn't.

Stated by the maker, publisher, or platform holder.

Tomorrow is Tim Cook's last day running Apple.

On 1 September, John Ternus — currently senior vice president of Hardware Engineering — becomes chief executive. Cook moves to executive chairman. It's the first CEO change at the world's most valuable consumer tech company since Cook took over from Steve Jobs in 2011, and it lands about two weeks before Apple's traditional September iPhone event.

This has been public since April, so it isn't a surprise. But the handover actually happening — versus being announced — is the moment worth marking, and there's a fair amount of confident nonsense circulating about what it means. Here's the confirmed part, then the reasonable reading.

What's confirmed

Apple announced the transition on 20 April 2026 through its own newsroom, so this is as solid as company news gets.

The handover

Cook's final day as CEO is 31 August 2026. Ternus takes over 1 September. Cook becomes executive chairman; Ternus joins the board the same day. The board approved it unanimously.

The mechanics beneath it, also from Apple: Arthur Levinson moves from non-executive chairman to lead independent director. Ternus's old hardware brief has already been split — Johny Srouji takes an expanded role, Tom Marieb takes the more direct hardware line. Those two changes took effect back in April, ahead of the top-level switch.

Cook, 65, isn't leaving the building. As executive chairman he keeps a specific job: engaging with policymakers worldwide. Apple said so explicitly, which matters — it means Cook stays the company's public face in Washington, Brussels and Beijing on tariffs, App Store regulation and chip supply, while Ternus runs the company.

Who Ternus is

Ternus, 50, joined Apple in 2001 on the product design team and became head of hardware engineering in

  1. If you've watched an Apple keynote in recent years you've seen him — he's fronted a lot of the

iPad and Mac hardware segments.

The detail that actually tells you something: he's a hardware engineer. Cook was an operations man — his 15 years were defined by supply-chain mastery, the services business crossing $100 billion a year, and the Mac's move to Apple silicon. Putting an engineer in the chair is a different bet, and it's a deliberate one.

At 50, he's the same age Cook was when he got the job. Apple clearly expects him to be there a decade or more.

What it signals — reasoning, not reporting

Here the confident takes start outrunning the facts, so labels matter.

The timing is the most-discussed point. Ternus takes over two weeks before the iPhone event, so his first keynote as CEO comes almost immediately. Apple made the effective date 1 September; the event is mid-September. That's tight, and it's presumably not an accident — but Apple hasn't said why, so anyone telling you the reasoning is guessing.

This is analysis

The read below is reasoning from Apple's own signals and past behaviour, not something the company has stated.

The plainest reading is continuity. This is an unusually long handover — four months from announcement to effect, longer than Microsoft's Ballmer-to-Nadella switch. Every Apple press release since April has still carried Cook's name as CEO. The company has spent four months telegraphing "nothing breaks," and keeping Cook on as chairman for policy work reinforces it.

The more interesting reading is the one Apple won't say out loud: a hardware engineer takes over precisely when Apple is seen to be behind on AI. Cook's era delivered extraordinary growth without breaking genuinely new ground in the way the Jobs era did. Handing the company to a product engineer, at the moment the industry has pivoted entirely to AI, is a bet that Apple's next phase is an engineering problem. Whether that's right is the whole question of Ternus's tenure, and no keynote will answer it quickly.

What it doesn't change

Not the September event — that was always happening, new CEO or not. Not the iPhone roadmap, which is set years ahead. Not, immediately, anything you can see as a customer.

The things worth watching aren't tomorrow's date. They're the first keynote, the first product cycle Ternus actually shapes rather than inherits, and how Apple's AI position looks a year from now. Those are the real tests, and they arrive slowly.

For now, one confirmed fact: on 1 September, for the first time in 15 years, someone other than Tim Cook runs Apple.

Update, 1 September

Apple has confirmed the event referenced here: it takes place on 9 September, and it will be Ternus's first keynote as CEO. Our preview of what's confirmed versus rumoured has the detail.
