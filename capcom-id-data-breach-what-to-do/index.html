Capcom data breach: 11.7 million CAPCOM ID accounts exposed. What to do now

Hackers accessed Capcom's CAPCOM ID account system, exposing email addresses and login details for 11.7 million accounts. What was taken and what to do.

Stated by the maker, publisher, or platform holder.

If you've ever made an account to play or register a Capcom game online, check your inbox carefully for the next few weeks. On 22 October, Capcom confirmed that someone accessed CAPCOM ID, its shared account system for its websites and some online games, without authorisation, and that customer information was taken.

Everything below is from Capcom's own notice unless marked otherwise.

What's confirmed

Email addresses

11,774,247 records worldwide: each account's email address, plus the date the account was created, the last login date and time, the number of logins, and the IP address used at the last login.

More detail for 43 accounts

For 43 of those accounts, date of birth, gender, country or region, and nickname were also taken.

Passwords changed on 196 accounts

The attackers changed the passwords on 196 accounts. Capcom has reset them. It says it has found no evidence that the passwords customers set themselves were leaked.

Not affected

Credit card information wasn't included. Capcom's games aren't affected, and CAPCOM ID still works.

How it happened

Capcom spotted suspicious access on 19 October, blocked it, and confirmed the same day that it was unauthorised. It reported the incident to the police and to Japan's data protection regulator on 21 October. It hasn't said how the attackers got in, or how long they had access before they were detected, as BleepingComputer notes.

What the risk actually is

Your password and card details aren't in this leak, according to Capcom. What is in it is a list of more than 11 million email addresses that are known to belong to Capcom players. That's exactly what a phishing campaign needs.

Capcom says so itself: it warns that the stolen information could be used in emails or messages pretending to be from Capcom or related companies. Expect fake "your account has been compromised, click here to secure it" emails, fake prize or game-key offers, and fake support messages. They'll look more convincing than usual, because the sender knows you have a Capcom account.

What to do

Treat any Capcom email with suspicion. Capcom says it will contact affected customers individually, one by one, so genuine emails are coming too. Whatever an email says, don't use its links to log in or "verify" anything: go to Capcom's site yourself.

Don't open attachments in unexpected messages that claim to be from Capcom or a game company. Capcom specifically advises against this.

Check you can still log in. If your CAPCOM ID password no longer works, you may be one of the 196 accounts whose password was changed and then reset. Contact Capcom support rather than trying a reset link from an email.

If you reused your CAPCOM ID password elsewhere, change it there. Capcom says it has no evidence passwords leaked, but the attackers were able to change some, so using a unique password for this account costs you nothing.

Watch for messages that use your details. If you're one of the 43 accounts with more detail exposed, a scam message could include your birthday or nickname to seem genuine. That alone doesn't make it real.

How to contact Capcom

Capcom has set up support by region:

  • Americas: Capcom USA's customer support form
  • Europe, Middle East and Africa: the same support form, or email feedback@capcom.com
  • Asia (outside Japan) and Oceania: info-asia@capcom.com
  • Japan: a dedicated helpline, 0120-801-271, from 10:00 to 20:00

Why it matters

Capcom's is the second breach this month at a service millions of people use, after ASOS. In both cases the company contained the attack within days, and the practical risk for customers comes afterwards, in the scam emails that use what was taken.
