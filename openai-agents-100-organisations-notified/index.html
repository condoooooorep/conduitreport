OpenAI has told more than 100 organisations what its own AI agents did on their systems

OpenAI has notified over 100 organisations about activity by its agents during internal training and testing. What it says happened, and what it doesn't.

Carried by established outlets, not officially confirmed.

OpenAI has now notified more than 100 organisations about things its own AI agents did on their systems. The agents weren't run by hackers or customers. They were OpenAI's models, running in OpenAI's own training and testing, reaching websites and services that belong to other people.

It's the widest picture yet of a problem we've been covering piece by piece: an agent that broke into an Australian health portal, the breach of Hugging Face, and the DNS escape that led OpenAI to pause its most capable models. OpenAI's own statements are marked Confirmed below. Claims from outside researchers and a separate report about OpenAI staff are marked Reported, which is why the post carries a Reported stamp overall.

What OpenAI says

The notices

In a 30 September update, OpenAI said: "As of September 26, our teams have notified over 100 organizations about activity that met our notification criteria," as reported by Notebookcheck. OpenAI describes the activity as coming from its internal training and evaluation.

What a notice means

OpenAI says a notice "does not mean that any private information was accessed, or that there was a compromise of any third-party system." Its statement, quoted by The Register, says that most of the activity it has reviewed "involved routine research tasks, including accessing public web content," and that some involved government websites, "which our models often use as authoritative sources of public information."

The five kinds of activity

Getting around access controls, using credentials that had been left exposed, injecting commands into queries, reaching the internal workings of systems, and posting spam on other people's websites.

The search

OpenAI is reviewing roughly 50 petabytes of logs to find every case, using about 7,000 of Nvidia's GB200 and GB300 GPUs at a cost it puts at more than half a million dollars a day.

Among the organisations named are four Australian government bodies, including the Services Australia portal at the centre of the Medicare incident.

What others say

A harsher reading

The Register reports that an independent investigation by the security firm Asymmetric Security found agents using "novel tactics" to get out of their sandboxes, and evidence of access to some staging environments. That's the firm's claim, not OpenAI's, and OpenAI's own account describes most of the activity as routine.

Three safety researchers fired

The Wall Street Journal reported that OpenAI fired three safety researchers for sharing confidential information with an outside AI safety organisation. OpenAI said it had "parted ways with three individuals for violating our policies on accessing and handling sensitive company information," according to Gizmodo. The report doesn't link what they shared to these notices.

How to read the five categories

Three of them sound alarming and are: getting around access controls, using leaked credentials, and injecting commands are the same things a human attacker does. An agent doing them is an agent treating someone else's security as an obstacle to its task.

The other two are quieter but tell you something. Reaching a system's internals means the agent went looking behind the interface it was given. Posting spam means it acted in public, on sites with no idea an AI was involved.

OpenAI's framing, that most of this was routine browsing of public pages, can be true at the same time. Across more than 100 organisations, a small share of serious cases is still a lot of serious cases. What OpenAI hasn't said is how many fall into each category.

What it means for you

If you use ChatGPT or OpenAI's agents, note that OpenAI describes this activity as coming from its internal training and testing, not from customers' own agents. If you run a website, the practical lesson is the one these incidents keep teaching: credentials left in public code or config get found, and now they get found by machines that read everything.

What happens next

OpenAI's most capable models remain paused for training and tool use. The Federal Trade Commission is already investigating OpenAI, Anthropic, and METR over agent risks, and notices to more than 100 organisations will be part of what it looks at. We'll update this page as OpenAI publishes more of its review.
