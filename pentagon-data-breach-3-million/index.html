Pentagon breach exposed about 3 million people's records: who's affected and what to do

A Pentagon records agency exposed names, Social Security numbers and birth dates of about 3 million people over nine months. What's confirmed, and what to do.

Stated by the maker, publisher, or platform holder.

The Pentagon has confirmed that personal records for more than 3 million people were exposed in a breach of the Defense Manpower Data Center, the agency that keeps personnel records for the US military. The data includes Social Security numbers.

Military Times first reported it on 24 September. Pentagon officials have since confirmed it to several outlets, and affected people are receiving notification letters.

What's confirmed

The scale

About 2.8 million living people and 294,000 people who have died. The DMDC holds records on troops and veterans, current and former civilian employees, contractors, and military family members, but officials haven't said which of those groups were affected.

The data

Names, dates of birth, Social Security numbers, contact information, and military job specialties, with other records varying by person.

The timeline

Unauthorised access ran from October 2025 to July 2026 through a vulnerability in a file-sharing system. The flaw was found and fixed on 16 July 2026. Notification letters are going out now, more than two months later.

The response

A Pentagon official said the DMDC "immediately initiated privacy and cybersecurity incident response actions." Affected people are being offered 12 months of free credit monitoring and identity restoration through IDX.

What isn't known

Officials declined to say who was behind it, whether the access was targeted, or why unencrypted personal data was reachable on that system. Nine months of access before anyone noticed is the part that will get the most scrutiny.

If you might be affected

If you've served, worked for the Defense Department, or are a military family member, watch for a letter. Then:

  1. Enrol in the IDX credit monitoring offered in the letter.
  2. Freeze your credit with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. It's free, and it makes it much harder for anyone to open new credit in your name.
  3. Be wary of calls, texts, and emails that mention your service record. Exposed data like this is often used to make scams sound official.

Active-duty service members and National Guard members can also get free electronic credit monitoring.

The bigger picture

This lands in the same week that a hacking group claimed it stole data on FBI agents and job applicants, and the same week we covered the Gyazo breach. Government systems holding decades of personnel records are as exposed as any company's, and a Social Security number, unlike a password, can't be changed.
