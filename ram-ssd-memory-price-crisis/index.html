Why your next PC, phone, and console just got more expensive — the memory crisis, explained

RAM prices are up over 100% and SSDs are climbing fast. It's not a shortage of chips — it's AI data centers eating the world's memory supply. What's confirmed.

Carried by established outlets, not officially confirmed.

If you've priced a RAM upgrade, an SSD, or a new laptop lately and flinched, you're not imagining it. The cost of memory has risen more sharply in 2026 than at almost any point in the industry's history — and the reason isn't a factory fire or a supply-chain snarl. It's AI.

This one carries a Reported stamp: the numbers come from market-research firms and manufacturer guidance rather than a single official source, and the exact figures vary by who's counting. But the direction is unambiguous and every serious tracker agrees on it.

What's happening

The price surge

Market researcher TrendForce reported PC DRAM contract prices rising more than 100% quarter-over-quarter entering 2026 — described as a record. Various trackers put DDR5 up anywhere from 90% to over 170% year-on-year, with SSD/NAND prices up 20–60% depending on category. A 32GB DDR5 kit that was roughly $79–149 in 2025 has been widely reported selling for $140–240.

How long it lasts

Analysts including Citigroup have suggested the imbalance could persist into 2027, with some forecasts stretching to 2028. SK Hynix has reportedly booked its entire 2026 high-bandwidth-memory capacity already.

The practical knock-on: PC makers including Dell and Lenovo have signalled price increases, IDC has forecast PC prices rising 8–15%, and at least one phone maker has warned of smartphone price rises tied to memory costs. Memory has gone from roughly 15% of a PC's build cost to as much as a third on some machines.

Why it's happening — and why it's different

This is not the 2020–2023 shortage. That one was pandemic supply-chain disruption — factories couldn't make enough. This is the opposite: factories are making plenty, but selling it to someone else.

That someone is the AI industry. Modern AI data centers consume enormous quantities of memory — both ordinary DRAM and specialised High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM) that feeds the GPUs running large models. HBM is the key. Each gigabyte of it reportedly consumes around three times the manufacturing wafer capacity of the regular DDR5 that goes in your laptop. So every time Samsung, SK Hynix or Micron devotes a production line to HBM for AI, far less consumer memory comes off the other end.

And the memory makers have every reason to do exactly that. HBM and enterprise memory are higher-margin products, and the AI firms buying them have effectively bottomless budgets — one estimate noted a single AI company's recent funding round exceeded the entire annual US computer market. Faced with a choice between selling to gamers or to hyperscalers, the manufacturers have chosen the hyperscalers.

This connects to everything else

The same AI-infrastructure boom driving Nvidia into your PC, driving Nvidia to buy Hugging Face, and driving OpenAI to build its own chips is the boom now inflating the price of the RAM in your desktop. It's the same story pushing outward into every corner of hardware.

What it means for you

If you're building or upgrading a PC: this is the worst time in years to buy RAM or an SSD, and it's not obviously going to improve soon. If your current machine is fine, waiting is defensible. If you must buy, buy only what you need rather than future-proofing with extra capacity you'll pay a premium for today.

If you're buying a laptop, phone, or prebuilt: expect prices up, and expect "same price, less RAM" to become common as makers protect margins by cutting specs instead of raising stickers. Check the memory and storage on a 2026 model against its 2025 equivalent before assuming it's the same deal.

If you're eyeing a console: this is worth watching closely given what's coming. Consoles run on the same memory, and a hardware launch into a memory-price spike is a genuine squeeze on the manufacturer. It's one more reason the timing of any new hardware over the next year is delicate.

The uncomfortable takeaway

The AI boom has mostly been an abstract thing for people who don't use the tools — a stream of headlines about models and valuations. This is where it reaches into an ordinary budget. The reason a stick of RAM costs double is that a data center in another state wanted it more, and could pay more. That's not a temporary glitch; it's what the current shape of the industry does to the price of everything with a memory chip in it, and the forecasts say it lasts into 2027 at least.

Because the figures here move monthly and come from trackers rather than one official source, treat the specific percentages as directional. The direction, unfortunately, is not in doubt.
