ChatGPT can now use your Chrome tabs: fill in forms, send messages, download files

OpenAI's updated ChatGPT extension for Chrome reads your open tabs and, on paid plans, can act in them. What it does, who gets it, and how to use it safely.

Carried by established outlets, not officially confirmed.

OpenAI has turned its ChatGPT extension for Chrome into something closer to an assistant that works in your browser for you. Announced on 15 October, the update lets ChatGPT read your open tabs and, in its Work mode, take actions in them: filling in forms, sending messages and downloading files.

It's the latest of the AI companies' "agents," tools that do tasks rather than just answer questions, to move into the browser, where most of people's accounts and personal information are.

What's confirmed

The extension

Published by OpenAI on the Chrome Web Store under the line "Control your browser with ChatGPT," updated on 15 October.

What it does

It can summarise and answer questions about any open tab, organise and group your tabs, and "take action in your tabs." OpenAI's examples include sending messages, filling out forms and downloading files, as 9to5Google reported.

How it runs

OpenAI says it works "in the background, without ever taking over your active browsing session," and "will always ask for your permission" before using your browser history or downloading or uploading files.

What's reported

Who gets what

Reading tabs and answering questions is rolling out to free and paid users. Acting on tabs, in Work mode, needs at least a Plus subscription, according to PiunikaWeb.

Other browsers

It also works in other browsers built on Chrome's code, such as Microsoft Edge, Brave and Opera.

What isn't clear

OpenAI hasn't said whether ChatGPT asks you to confirm before it sends a message or submits a form. One of its own example requests tells ChatGPT to fill in a registration form "and stop before submitting." That suggests stopping is something you ask for, not something it does by default.

OpenAI also hasn't published how it tests the extension against prompt injection: instructions hidden in a web page that try to make an AI agent do something its user didn't ask for. That's the main known risk with any AI that reads and acts on web pages, and it matters more when the AI can use sites you're signed in to.

How to use it safely

Say where to stop. For anything that sends, submits or pays, tell ChatGPT to stop before the final step, and do that step yourself.

Close sensitive tabs first. Banking, email and work accounts don't need to be open while ChatGPT is working in your browser.

Check what it did. Read the message before it's sent, or the form before it's submitted, and look at what was downloaded.

Start with low-stakes tasks. Summarising and organising tabs carries little risk. Acting on your accounts is where mistakes cost something.

Why it matters

Agents acting in places they weren't meant to has been one of the year's running stories, from OpenAI notifying 100 organisations about its agents' behaviour to GitHub's Copilot taking over the mouse and keyboard. Putting an agent inside the browser most people already use, with their accounts already signed in, is a big step in bringing that to ordinary users. It also comes a week after GPT-6 reached free ChatGPT users.
