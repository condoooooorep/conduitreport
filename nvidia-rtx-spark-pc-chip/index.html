Nvidia wants to build the whole PC now, and Intel and AMD noticed

The RTX Spark superchip puts Nvidia in the Windows PC business it never competed in. The chip is confirmed. The price and ship date still aren't.

Stated by the maker, publisher, or platform holder.

For thirty years, the brains of a Windows PC came from Intel or AMD. Nvidia made the graphics card that plugged in alongside. That arrangement is what Nvidia is now trying to end.

At Computex in June, Nvidia unveiled the RTX Spark — a "superchip" that combines an Arm-based CPU and a Blackwell GPU into a single platform built to power Windows laptops and compact desktops directly. Jensen Huang's framing was blunt: this is going to "reinvent the PC." The company has spent every month since confirming one more piece of it, and reaffirmed the autumn roadmap as recently as this month.

The significance isn't the specs. It's that Nvidia is entering a business it has never directly competed in — selling the core platform of a PC, not just the graphics inside it.

What's confirmed

The chip exists and is coming

Nvidia announced RTX Spark at Computex 2026 and has reaffirmed a fall 2026 window. At full strength it pairs a 20-core Arm CPU with a Blackwell GPU (6,144 CUDA cores), up to 128GB of unified memory, and a claimed 1 petaflop of FP4 AI performance — enough, Nvidia says, to run 120-billion-parameter models locally.

The headline capability is that last part: running large AI models on the laptop itself, without sending anything to the cloud. That's the pitch — an "AI PC" where the intelligence is local. Whether people need that is a separate question from whether Nvidia can build it, and Nvidia clearly can.

What is not confirmed

The price

Figures around $1,799 for entry configurations are circulating, but these are PC-maker estimates, not Nvidia pricing. Nvidia has pointedly not published official prices for any tier.

The tiers

Reporting describes four silicon tiers, with only the top one reaching the full 128GB of memory — the lower three cap at 64GB. That comes from teardown reporting, not an Nvidia spec sheet.

There's also no firm ship date. "This fall" has not been sharpened into a day. Nvidia's pattern since June has been to confirm one component at a time while holding back the two things buyers care about most — price and availability — until closer to launch.

Why Intel and AMD stock fell

The market read it immediately. When Nvidia announced its PC-chip entry, shares of AMD, Intel, and Qualcomm dropped, while Dell — expected to build RTX Spark machines — jumped. Intel fell over 4%.

The reason is structural. Intel and AMD's core business is the x86 CPU that has defined Windows PCs for decades. Nvidia arriving with an Arm-based alternative, backed by Microsoft and its own dominance in AI silicon, is a direct threat to the one market Nvidia wasn't already winning. Nvidia already owns the data centre. This is the company pushing to the "edge" — the devices in front of people — and owning every layer of the stack.

What it means for you

Nothing immediately, and possibly quite a lot within a year.

If you buy a Windows laptop, there may soon be a third kind of chip inside it, one built around AI running locally rather than in the cloud. Whether that's worth paying for depends entirely on whether local AI turns out to be something you use — which nobody can honestly answer yet, because the hardware to try it at scale isn't in people's hands.

The safe read: the chip is real and capable, the strategy is serious, and the two facts that decide whether it matters to a normal buyer — what it costs and when you can get one — are exactly the two Nvidia hasn't released. Watch for those.
